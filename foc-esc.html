Sensorless Field-Oriented Control ESC

STM32G4 · Bare-metal·DRV8302 · SVPWM · Sensorless FOC·Altium · Simulink · 2025 – Present

A custom STM32G4-based electronic speed controller (ESC) that runs sensorless field-oriented control on a BLDC motor. The 4-layer board carries a 12 V / 30 A three-phase MOSFET power stage with reverse-polarity and TVS protection, an on-board 3.3 V logic supply, and full current/voltage/temperature sensing. Firmware is written in PlatformIO with STM32CubeMX peripheral setup, and the control law is validated in a Simulink model before hardware bring-up.

System architecture

The board is organized around one power path and one control path. 12 V comes in through protection and a filtered DC-link, then splits: a buck converter powers the 3.3 V logic and the STM32G4, while the DC-link feeds the three-phase inverter that actually drives the motor. The MCU closes the loop — it commands the gate driver with PWM and reads back phase currents, bus/phase voltages, and back-EMF to know where the rotor is.

How the control works

Field-oriented control treats the three motor currents as a single rotating vector and splits it into two components in the rotor frame: torque-producing current (q-axis) and flux-producing current (d-axis). By regulating those two independently, the controller commands smooth torque instead of the coarse six-step commutation a simpler ESC would use.

  • Sensorless position. There is no Hall sensor or encoder. Rotor angle and speed are recovered from the motor’s own back-EMF — the three phase voltages are measured against a virtual neutral and the zero-crossings of the back-EMF waveform mark known electrical positions, which the estimator turns into a continuous angle for the Park transforms.
  • Clarke & Park transforms. The two measured phase currents are projected from the stationary three-phase frame into the two-axis (αβ) frame and then rotated into the rotor (dq) frame, so the controller works with slow-moving DC-like quantities instead of AC waveforms.
  • d/q PI regulators. Independent PI loops hold the d-axis current near zero (no wasted flux) and drive the q-axis current to the torque command.
  • Inverse Park & SVPWM. The controller output is rotated back to the stationary frame and turned into three PWM duty cycles by space-vector modulation, which the gate driver applies to the six MOSFETs.

Power input & protection

Everything downstream depends on a clean, protected 12 V rail, so the input stage handles transients, reverse polarity, and switching noise before the DC-link.

  • TVS transient clamp. An SMBJ30CA bidirectional TVS sits across the input. It stays non-conductive up to 30 V, begins clamping around 33 V, and holds spikes to roughly 48.4 V by shunting the surge to ground.
  • Reverse-polarity protection. A low-side IRFR7440 N-channel MOSFET blocks reverse connection. N-channel is chosen for its lower RDS(on) (and thus lower loss) versus a P-channel part; a 16 V gate–source Zener clamps the gate and a series resistor limits inrush.
  • π-filter & capacitor bank. A 100 µF / 1 µH / 300 µF low-pass π-filter attenuates above ~10 kHz, and extra bulk capacitance on the output supports the transient current demanded by high-speed MOSFET switching.

Logic supply

The 3.3 V rail for the STM32G4 and surrounding logic comes from a TPS54331 buck converter rated to 3 A, following the datasheet reference design. Two tweaks make it robust for bench work:

  • Dual-source input. A Schottky diode-OR feeds the converter’s VIN from whichever is higher — the 12 V main input or 5 V USB — so the board can be programmed and debugged from USB even when the main supply is off, while the diodes block reverse current between sources.
  • EN clamp. A resistor divider plus Zener clamps the 12 V rail down to a safe level for the converter’s enable pin.

Three-phase power stage

The motor is driven by a three-phase inverter of six external low-RDS(on) N-channel MOSFETs, switched by a DRV8302 gate driver. The MCU sends PWM to the DRV8302, which drives the three half-bridges; phase current is sensed on the low side of two phases through shunts and the driver’s integrated current-sense amplifiers. The DRV8302’s own buck regulator is disabled since the board already has its dedicated logic supply.

Sensing & feedback

Good control needs good measurements. The board senses current, several voltages, temperature, and back-EMF, conditioning each to land inside the MCU’s 0–3.3 V ADC range.

  • Phase current. Dual low-side shunts on the U and V phases feed the DRV8302’s integrated current-sense amplifiers — the two measurements the FOC transforms need.
  • Total DC current, shunt-less. Rather than adding a dedicated sense resistor, the board reuses the reverse-polarity MOSFET’s RDS(on) as the sense element: a differential amplifier measures the small drop from GND to VBAT_GND across it, with a differential-mode input filter and an output low-pass filter before the ADC.
Gain = Vo,max / (RDS(on) × Imax) = 3.2 V / (0.0024 Ω × 30 A) ≈ 44.4  — realized with R₃ = 270 Ω, R₄ = 12 kΩ on the differential amp.
  • Voltage sensing. The battery, DC-link, and three phase voltages are each read through resistor dividers with decoupling capacitors, and Schottky diodes clamp every ADC input to 3.3 V against overvoltage.
  • Back-EMF for sensorless control. The three phase voltages are measured against a virtual neutral so the controller can detect back-EMF zero-crossings and recover rotor position.
  • Thermal protection. Inverter temperature is monitored so the firmware can fault before the MOSFETs overheat.

MCU interfaces & peripherals

The STM32G4 sits at the center with the interfaces needed to program, tune, and monitor the ESC:

  • SWD for programming and real-time debugging.
  • USB 2.0 full-speed for a PC-based GUI, data logging, and powering/debugging the board when the main supply is off.
  • Auxiliary UART for debug output and talking to external devices.
  • Analog input channel for an external command signal or sensor.
  • User I/O: NRST, a general-purpose button, and a spare button; fault / gate-enable / power status LEDs.
  • 8 KB I²C EEPROM for non-volatile configuration and calibration storage.

  • Power/signal separation. High-current 12 V traces run mainly on the bottom layer as wide, direct paths, while all low-voltage digital and control circuitry lives in the top-left quadrant, physically away from the power stage to minimize EMI.
  • Controlled routing. Differential pairs are routed where signal integrity matters, and copper pours carry the high-current paths to support 30 A while keeping IR losses low.
  • In progress. Via stitching between the ground and power planes, and trace width/spacing tuned to IPC-2221A and thermal targets around the switching nodes and MOSFETs, with polygon prioritization and thermal reliefs in Altium.

Simulation & status

Before firmware bring-up, the control law is exercised in a Simulink model of the three-phase inverter and FOC chain — motor control, the transforms, and space-vector modulation — alongside models of the supporting circuitry (TVS, MOSFETs, reverse-polarity protection, buck converter, RC snubbers, and the op-amp sense chain) used to size components against the real design.

Status: PCB layout is being finalized and the Simulink control model refined ahead of hardware bring-up.
